      What this does

      I run streams for events, and every time I end up with five tabs open: YouTube's encoder page, Meta's, LinkedIn's, a speed test, and a calculator to figure out how big the recordings will be. This puts it in one place. The live stream planner gives you each platform's bitrate, the total you push for a simulcast, and the upload speed to ask the venue for. The video file size calculator handles recordings, target sizes and how many drives to bring. The upload time calculator tells you if the footage will be on Dropbox before the client asks.

      How to use it

      1. Add your platforms. One row per destination. Add more to see the simulcast total.
      2. Pick resolution, frame rate and codec. Only options the platform accepts are listed.
      3. Choose headroom and type your measured upload. Use 2× unless you have a dedicated wired line.
      4. Copy the settings. The OBS and vMix hints match the numbers, and Copy summary gives you text for the run sheet.

      How much upload speed do you need for streaming?

      Your stream is video bitrate plus audio bitrate. Your connection has to carry that all the time, not on average, and real connections wobble. So the rule is:

      Upload needed = (video + audio) × 1.5 at minimum, × 2 to be safe.

      1.5× is fine on a wired line nobody else is using. 2× is what I use for anything with a client attached, and it's what Microsoft's own Teams encoder guide says: double the streaming bitrate when you size bandwidth. Vimeo says the same thing more vaguely: keep the bitrate well under your upload speed. Venue Wi-Fi with 300 attendees on it is not your upload speed, whatever the speed test said at 8 a.m.

      Simulcasting from one encoder multiplies the upload: YouTube at 6 Mbps plus Facebook at 4.5 Mbps plus LinkedIn at 3.5 Mbps is 14 Mbps of video before headroom. Sending one stream to a relay service and letting it fan out costs only one stream of upload.

      FAQ

      What bitrate should I use for YouTube Live at 1080p?

      YouTube's 2026 guidance for H.264 is a minimum of 5 Mbps and a recommended 14 Mbps at 1080p 30 fps, and 6 to 17 Mbps at 1080p 60 fps. With HEVC or AV1 it drops to 4 to 10 Mbps at 30 fps and 4 to 12 Mbps at 60 fps. For most event streams on a normal venue line, something around 6 to 8 Mbps at 1080p30 looks clean.

      How much upload speed do I need for streaming?

      Add up the video and audio bitrate of everything you send, then multiply by 1.5 at the very least, and by 2 for anything that matters. A 6 Mbps stream needs 9 to 12 Mbps of real, measured upload. Microsoft's Teams encoder guide gives the same advice: double your streaming bitrate when sizing bandwidth.

      Why do platforms ask for CBR and a 2 second keyframe interval?

      Constant bitrate keeps the data rate steady so it does not spike past your connection during fast motion. A keyframe every 2 seconds lets the platform cut your stream into segments and switch viewers between qualities cleanly. YouTube and Facebook say never go over 4 seconds.

      Can I stream to YouTube, Facebook and LinkedIn at the same time?

      Yes. Either your encoder sends a separate stream to each platform, which multiplies your upload, or you send one stream to a relay service that forwards it, which only costs one stream of upload. Watch platform caps: LinkedIn tops out at 6 Mbps and 30 fps, Twitch at 6 Mbps on standard ingest.

      Should I stream in HEVC or AV1 instead of H.264?

      Only where the platform accepts it. YouTube Live takes H.264, HEVC and AV1, and its recommended bitrates for HEVC and AV1 are roughly 30% lower. Twitch takes HEVC for 1440p through Enhanced Broadcasting. Facebook, LinkedIn, Vimeo and Teams RTMP-In want H.264.

      How big is a one hour 1080p video file?

      At a typical H.264 delivery bitrate of 8 Mbps plus 384 kbps audio, one hour is about 3.8 GB. The same hour in ProRes 422 HQ at 1080p 30 fps is about 99 GB, and in 4K ProRes 422 HQ about 398 GB.

      Why does my computer show a smaller file size than the calculator?

      Drive makers and this calculator use decimal units, where 1 GB is 1,000,000,000 bytes. Windows uses binary units, where its GB is really 1,073,741,824 bytes, so the same file looks about 7% smaller. The calculator shows both.

      Does this calculator send my numbers anywhere?

      No. Everything runs in your browser. Nothing you type leaves the page, and there is no tracking. Your last settings are saved in your own browser so they are there next time.
